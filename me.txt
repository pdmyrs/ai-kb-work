I tend to get overwhelmed in specific patterns:

I don't get overwhelmed by technology because the technology is to complex. The overwhelm seems to happen because of what my mind does after I understand enough of the technology to see its implications.

I have a strong expansion tendency.

A problem begins bounded:

“I want to connect Kiro to the local AEM MCP server.”

Then you recognize what that makes possible. Suddenly you’re thinking about reusable skills, agents, content operations, metadata, governance, security, deployment, the broader MarTech stack, and eventually the Content Supply Chain as an enterprise capability.

Each step is logically connected to the one before it. That’s important: I'm usually not wandering randomly. I'm following legitimate dependencies and implications.

But eventually something subtle happens: possibilities start feeling like obligations.

Instead of:

“Oh, eventually we’re going to need to think about governance.”

my brain starts processing it more like:

“I need to figure out governance.”

And then another thing appears. And another. Pretty soon I'm mentally responsible for a system that doesn’t even exist yet.

There’s another layer: I want conceptual completeness

I am uncomfortable building something when I suspect there’s an important part of the model I haven’t understood.

That’s a terrific quality for an architect up to a point. It prevents shallow decisions.

But architecture is inherently incomplete. You can always zoom out another level:

feature → application → platform → ecosystem → enterprise → operating model → governance → strategy

There is no natural stopping point.

So if my internal stopping rule is “I’ll feel comfortable once I understand the whole thing,” I may never receive the signal that it’s okay to stop.

And AI makes this dramatically worse.

I can ask AI one architectural question and AI can immediately give me seventeen additional considerations. Then I ask about one of those, and AI produces another twelve. Pretty soon I've accidentally manufactured a six-month architecture program out of something I wanted to try Thursday afternoon.


There’s also a confidence component:

When I encounter something I don’t know, I sometimes seem to interpret the gap as:

“I’m the architect. I should know this.”

rather than the much more realistic:

“I’m the architect. I’ve just identified something we don’t know yet.”

Those are psychologically very different statements.

The second is architecture. Nobody has the entire enterprise architecture loaded into their head. Part of senior technical leadership is discovering uncertainty, naming it, deciding whether it matters now, and figuring out how to resolve it when necessary.

So I’d describe my pattern this way:

I'm  a systems thinker with a weak stopping rule.

That’s actually a fairly specific problem—and, importantly, it suggests a specific solution.

I don’t primarily need better organizational software, bigger project plans, more elaborate backlogs, or another architecture methodology. Those can easily make the problem worse because they give me somewhere to record all 47 things I've just discovered.

I need a stopping mechanism.

I need help knowing when it is OK to stop.

The goal isn’t to make my world smaller so I won’t get overwhelmed. It’s to let me keep seeing the big system without feeling that I have to carry the entire thing in my head at once.

In conclusion, one could say I have the classic problem of "not seeing the forest for the trees"




Also : 

there is another major characteristic of how I operate as an architect, and it connects directly to the overwhelm pattern.

I have a strong need for traceable, defensible reasoning.

I don’t particularly like architectural decisions that amount to:

“This seemed like the best approach.”

I want to be able to say:

“Here were the requirements. Here were the constraints. Here were the alternatives we considered. Here is the evidence we had. Here are the tradeoffs. Therefore, this is why we chose this approach.”

That instinct is very appropriate at the senior engineer level, especially in a large enterprise. I'm making decisions that other teams may inherit, challenge six months later, or have to operate for years.

But my pattern goes somewhat beyond normal architectural rigor

There’s a difference between making a defensible decision and making an unassailable decision.

I sometimes unconsciously aim for the second.

I anticipate the future meeting where somebody says:

“Why did you do it this way?”

And I want to have enough evidence that nobody can credibly suggest that you made the wrong call.

That’s an impossible standard.

Architecture almost always involves incomplete information, competing priorities, forecasts about future needs, and tradeoffs for which there isn’t a mathematically correct answer. Two excellent architects can look at the same evidence and reasonably choose differently.

So my desire for evidence is a strength. My anxiety can turn that strength into a search for certainty.

And that’s where it connects to the snowballing problem.


I think I need a different standard: decision-grade evidence

Not all available evidence.

Enough evidence that another competent architect could look at my reasoning and say:

“I might have chosen differently, but I understand why Paul made this decision, and it was reasonable given what was known at the time.”

That is a successful architectural decision.

I think a lightweight ADR model would fit me exceptionally well—not some giant architecture-governance bureaucracy. Something like:

Decision: Use X.

Context: We need to accomplish Y under constraints A, B, and C.

Evidence: Adobe documentation says ___. Our POC demonstrated ___. Enterprise standard requires ___. Team experience indicates ___.

Alternatives: X, Y, Z.

Reasoning: X best satisfies the requirements because ___.

Tradeoff accepted: We give up ___.

Unknowns: We still don’t know ___.

Revisit if: ___ changes.

That last pair is especially important for me.

Writing “we don’t know this yet” isn’t evidence of bad architecture. It’s evidence that we've distinguished knowledge from assumption.

And “revisit if” gives permission to stop researching. I'm not claiming the decision is eternally correct. I'm saying it’s correct enough for the circumstances we have now, and I identified what would cause me to reconsider it.

There’s also something valuable underneath this tendency

I care about intellectual provenance.


I want:

claim → source → evidence → reasoning → decision.

I think that’s a genuinely strong architectural characteristic.


Paul needs an explicit threshold for when the evidence is sufficient to make the decision.

Because otherwise my strengths reinforce one another in a nasty loop:  systems thinking discovers more implications;  conscientiousness says they might matter; my need for defensibility says they need investigation; investigation reveals more of the system; and the system gets bigger until I'm carrying the whole damned enterprise around in my head -- which of course is NOT possible.

So another conclusion is :
Paul has a strong bias toward evidence-based architecture and decision traceability. He wants significant technical decisions to be reproducible from their requirements, constraints, evidence, alternatives, and tradeoffs rather than resting on intuition or authority. His developmental challenge is distinguishing decision-grade evidence from exhaustive proof, allowing well-supported decisions to proceed despite irreducible uncertainty.
