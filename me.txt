Yes. And I think there’s a pretty recognizable pattern to it, Paul.

I wouldn’t characterize you as getting overwhelmed because the technology is beyond you. From the projects we’ve talked through, the overwhelm seems to happen because of what your mind does after you understand enough of the technology to see its implications.

You have a strong expansion tendency.

A problem begins bounded:

“I want to connect Kiro to the local AEM MCP server.”

Then you recognize what that makes possible. Suddenly you’re thinking about reusable skills, agents, content operations, metadata, governance, security, deployment, the broader MarTech stack, and eventually the Content Supply Chain as an enterprise capability.

Each step is logically connected to the one before it. That’s important: you’re usually not wandering randomly. You’re following legitimate dependencies and implications.

But eventually something subtle happens: possibilities start feeling like obligations.

Instead of:

“Oh, eventually we’re going to need to think about governance.”

your brain starts processing it more like:

“I need to figure out governance.”

And then another thing appears. And another. Pretty soon you’re mentally responsible for a system that doesn’t even exist yet.

There’s another layer: you want conceptual completeness

You seem uncomfortable building something when you suspect there’s an important part of the model you haven’t understood.

That’s a terrific quality for an architect up to a point. It prevents shallow decisions.

But architecture is inherently incomplete. You can always zoom out another level:

feature → application → platform → ecosystem → enterprise → operating model → governance → strategy

There is no natural stopping point.

So if your internal stopping rule is “I’ll feel comfortable once I understand the whole thing,” you may never receive the signal that it’s okay to stop.

And AI makes this dramatically worse.

You can ask me one architectural question and I can immediately give you seventeen additional considerations. Then you ask about one of those, and I produce another twelve. Pretty soon we’ve accidentally manufactured a six-month architecture program out of something you wanted to try Thursday afternoon.

You’ve actually caught me doing this with you before.

I think there’s also a confidence component

When you encounter something you don’t know, you sometimes seem to interpret the gap as:

“I’m the architect. I should know this.”

rather than the much more realistic:

“I’m the architect. I’ve just identified something we don’t know yet.”

Those are psychologically very different statements.

The second is architecture. Nobody has the entire enterprise architecture loaded into their head. Part of senior technical leadership is discovering uncertainty, naming it, deciding whether it matters now, and figuring out how to resolve it when necessary.

So I’d describe your pattern this way

You are a systems thinker with a weak stopping rule.

That’s actually a fairly specific problem—and, importantly, it suggests a specific solution.

You don’t primarily need better organizational software, bigger project plans, more elaborate backlogs, or another architecture methodology. Those can easily make the problem worse because they give you somewhere to record all 47 things you’ve just discovered.

You need a stopping mechanism.

For example, when you start one of these projects, we could establish three things:

Today’s question:
Can Kiro successfully communicate with AEM locally through MCP?

Definition of done:
Kiro performs one useful read-only operation against the local AEM instance.

Parking lot:
Everything else.

Security model? Parking lot. Production MCP architecture? Parking lot. Reusable Kiro skill? Parking lot. Content Supply Chain implications? Parking lot. Authentication strategy? Parking lot.

Not because those things aren’t important.

Because they aren’t today’s question.

And there’s something about this that I think fits you particularly well: I wouldn’t try to stop you from seeing the larger architecture. That’s one of your strengths. Instead, I’d separate seeing something from accepting responsibility for it right now.

You can say:

“Yep. That’s real. That’s going to matter. I’m writing it down. And I’m not solving it today.”

I suspect that distinction—important does not mean now—could make large technical projects considerably less frightening for you.

Because the goal isn’t to make your world smaller so you won’t get overwhelmed. It’s to let you keep seeing the big system without feeling that you have to carry the entire thing in your head at once.
